When you think about the United Kingdom, at least for me as someone who loves history,
it's hard not to think about the Royal Navy. The powerful British Navy that, for centuries,
helped the United Kingdom build an empire that stretched across nearly every continent.
An empire so vast that it gave rise to that famous phrase,
the sun never sets on the British Empire. In Tolkien's world, there was Númenor,
a great island in the middle of the ocean separated from the rest of Middle Earth,
yet destined to become one of the most powerful civilizations of its age.
A relatively small island, compared to an entire continent,
but one with enormous naval and political influence. Did Tolkien look at British history
and draw some inspiration from it? Well, I don't know, but it is an interesting comparison.
Because for a very long time, a relatively small island off the coast of Europe managed
to build the most powerful navy on Earth and project its power into almost every corner
of the world. But of course, that world is gone. The question is, how much of that power is still
there today? And the truth is, the Royal Navy still belongs to a very exclusive group.
It remains one of the few navies in the world capable of assembling a carrier strike group
and sending it thousands of miles away from home. At the center of that capability are
HMS Queen Elizabeth and HMS Prince of Wales, the largest warships ever built for the Royal
Navy. And when one of these carriers deploys on a full-scale operation, it doesn't go alone.
There are F-35B fighters, Merlin helicopters, escort ships, support vessels, and an entire
structure built around command and control, air defense, and anti-submarine warfare.
That is a level of capability that very few navies can put to sea today.
In 2025, for example, the carrier strike group led by Prince of Wales deployed on
Operation Highmast. The mission lasted around eight months, taking the group through the
Mediterranean and all the way into the Indo-Pacific. During the final phase of the deployment,
back in the Mediterranean, the carrier had 24 British F-35Bs on board. At that point,
it was the largest number of F-35Bs ever embarked on either of Britain's two Queen
Elizabeth class carriers. And that deployment revealed two important things about the Royal
Navy today. First, the United Kingdom is still capable of organizing and leading a complex
naval operation on the other side of the world. That is no small achievement. It requires logistics,
maintenance, fuel, ammunition, escorts, and the ability to keep everything operating for months
at a time, thousands of miles from home. But the second point is just as important.
Britain increasingly does this alongside its allies. The original formation, for example,
included frigates from Norway and Canada, as well as a Norwegian support ship. That cooperation
strengthens the group's defenses, improves logistics, and spreads some of the burden across
several countries. But that doesn't automatically mean the United Kingdom is incapable of
conducting an operation on its own. To understand how much independence Britain actually has,
we need to look at another question. How many British ships are really available?
And more importantly, how long can the country sustain an operation of this size before it
starts putting serious pressure on the rest of the fleet?
